## 1. Question and goal

**Entry point (the interest).** How elders' culture and sense of agency do, or don't, turn into a community role.

**Research question.**
> At two culturally different San Francisco senior centers that offer the same services, how do elders with and without a community role describe their culture and their own agency?

**Goal.**
> Document, in elders' own words, how a sense of cultural strength and a sense of agency appear together, and what it takes for them to become a community role.

**Institutional sub-question (Layer B).**
> What channels do Dongmyo and the SF Bay Area give elders to take on a role, and who asks?

**Stance.** Qualitative, descriptive and interpretive, not causal. I start from the interest, not from a claim to confirm. The propositions in section 5 tell me where to look and can be wrong.

**Film, in one line.** The same question, shown to a public audience, with an open ending.

## 3. Borrowing from Dongmyo (last year's project)

**Last year.** Dongmyo, Jongno, Seoul. Team #9 with Daria, 15 weeks. I asked which elderly groups are most susceptible to isolation and what limits their well-being. I proposed the *Dongmyo Role Ecology Grassroots Program*: peer "anchors" offering small roles in three phases (personal care, community roles, economic engagement).

**What worked.** Staff interviews, observation, institution-first access with snowball referrals.
**What didn't.** Door-to-door outreach, slow institutional replies, distrust and stigma, direct "mental health" questions.

**What I'm borrowing**
1. **Institution first, snowball second.** A short written plan (affiliation, course, instructor, purpose, method, participants, schedule, ethics), then referrals from a trusted staff member.
2. **Indirect, experience-based questions** ("where do you spend time?"), not "are you lonely?" Real content tends to come on the third or fourth meeting.
3. **The role lens.** Not feeling needed matters as much as not meeting people.
4. **The "anchor" idea.** My Channel 1 (a peer offers a role) is what the volunteering literature calls *being asked*.
5. **Gender as an axis.** Men's non-family networks shrink at retirement; women keep neighborhood ties but carry money and caregiving strain. I never used it. Record it this time.

**What I'm fixing**
1. Most interview evidence came from non-elders. This time, elders' own accounts.
2. I assumed elders lacked motivation. Now want and have are measured separately, and non-wanting counts as a legitimate finding.
3. Several numbers went further than the sources allowed. Every figure now carries a status tag.
4. I never compared my idea with what already exists. That is now Layer B.

## 4. Concepts and what anchors them

| Concept | Working definition | Anchor | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Role** | A recurring, named responsibility others rely on, formal or informal (lunch table, class, translating, minding grandchildren, looking out for neighbors) | Role-identity absence and volunteering (Greenfield & Marks 2004); role-identity theory (Thoits) | ✅ / ◇ |
| **Asked** | Someone (staff, peer, family) explicitly invited the person to take something on | Recruitment as a reason for participation: people don't participate because they *can't*, *don't want to*, or *nobody asked* (Verba, Schlozman & Brady 1995). Older volunteers: 63% asked by an organization, 24% by friends (SSWR 2005) | ✅ / ◻ |
| **Agency** | Acting with intention, planning, weighing options, drawing on past skills, including *relational* agency | Emirbayer & Mische 1998: iterational (past skills), projective (plans), practical-evaluative (judgment in the moment). Bandura 2001: personal, **proxy** and collective agency, which fits "deciding with family" | ✅ / ◇ |
| **Cultural strength** | Felt belonging and commitment, pride, everyday practice (language, food, ritual), being a carrier of culture for the young | Ethnic identity and well-being (Smith & Silva 2011); generativity (Erikson); strengths-based "community cultural wealth" (Yosso 2005) | ✅ / ◇ |
| **Connected non-taker** | Regular attendee with no role, by their own and staff account | Older adults narrow networks by choice (Carstensen's socioemotional selectivity); continuity theory (Atchley 1989) | ✅ / ◇ |
| **Culture shapes the *form* of roles** | Family-first expectations can make informal or family roles the main route | Diversity 2010 review; Xu et al. 2018; Markus & Kitayama 1991 and Kağıtçıbaşı 2005 on culture and the self | ◻ / ◇ |

**Caveat on the "asked" anchor.** Verba et al. studied *political* participation. Applying it to senior-center roles is an extension by analogy, not a tested result. The volunteering literature (Musick & Wilson; SSWR 2005) is the closer evidence.

## 5. Working propositions (replacing H1–H3)

Propositions guide what to look for in a multiple-case design (Yin ◇). They can be wrong, and a case that breaks one is the most useful case.

| | Proposition | Looks like support | Looks like challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| **P1** Co-occurrence | Rich accounts of cultural practice, belonging, or passing culture on often appear alongside accounts of planning, choice, or initiative | Both kinds of talk appear together in most cases | Strong cultural talk with little initiative (or the reverse) in about as many cases as not |
| **P2** Channel | That link becomes a *role* mainly where the site (or family) offers a channel: someone asks, or a program fits the elder's language and culture. Elsewhere it shows as informal or family roles | Role-takers describe a specific ask or channel. Strong-culture non-takers say nobody asked, nothing fit, or show informal roles | Roles with no channel or ask; or channels exist and strong-culture elders decline for unrelated reasons |
| **P3** Rival | Health, being asked, or simply not wanting a role explains the pattern better than culture | The split disappears once I sort by want, asked, health | The split persists among elders who want a role, were asked, and are healthy |

**How I code "strong" without imposing it.** I code *kinds* of talk, not levels.
- Cultural talk: practice, pride, belonging, transmission, tension or loss.
- Agency talk: iterational, projective, practical-evaluative; personal, proxy (family) or collective.
- Then I look at co-occurrence case by case in a matrix.

**What this study will not claim**
- Causation.
- That findings hold for Chinese or Filipino elders in general.
- Anything about isolated elders. This is a study of elders *connected* to a center.

**Honest reading**
1. **No source tests the cultural-strength ↔ agency ↔ role link.** The literature supports each piece (roles help the role-absent; being asked matters; agency has a workable framework; culture matters modestly). The *connection* is the gap, which is why the question is worth asking and why the design is exploratory. The literature supports the direction, not the answer.
2. **Strongest anchors:** role-absence (✅), being asked (✅ as framework), the agency triad (✅).
3. **Weakest anchor:** the cultural effect. r = .17, weaker after 40 (✅). Expect subtlety, and count informal roles.
4. **Two stretches to avoid**
   - **(a)** The 3.7% figure shows *neighborhood explains little*. It does not show that the *site* explains much. Site choice is justified by controlling the service bundle, not by that number.
   - **(b)** Korea's 2.5% volunteering and California's 20.6% are **not comparable** without checking definitions, wording and age ranges. Put them in separate sentences as context, never as a contrast.

## 7. Layer A: elders in San Francisco

### 7.1 Site logic

DAS's Service Site Guide (FY2025–26) lists what each center offers.

| Site | Dist. | Provider | DAS services listed | Cultural read (verify) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lady Shaw Senior Center, 1483 Mason St | D3 | Self-Help for the Elderly (SHE) | Community service center · congregate meals · intergenerational · SF Connected | Chinatown; presumably Chinese-speaking |
| Manilatown Senior Center 中菲康樂中心, 848 Kearny St #306 | D3 | SHE | Community service center · congregate meals · fitness (On Lok) · intergenerational | Inside I-Hotel senior housing; Chinese name reads "Chinese–Filipino". Get the actual split |
| Geen Mun Senior Center, 777 Stockton St | D3 | SHE | Resource center · community service center · congregate meals · intergenerational · naturalization · escorts | Chinatown |
| Jackie Chan Senior Center, 5757 Geary Blvd | D1 | SHE | Community service center · congregate meals · intergenerational · naturalization · escorts | Outer Richmond; mix unknown |
| Richmond Senior Center, 6221 Geary Blvd | D1 | Golden Gate Senior Services | Resource center · community service center · congregate meals · fitness · SF Connected · Village | Richmond; more general |
| Russian American Community Services, 300 Anza St | D2 | RACS | Community service center · congregate meals | Russian-speaking |
| Western Addition Senior Center, 1390½ Turk St | D5 | Bayview Hunters Point Multipurpose Senior Services | Resource center · case management · community service center · congregate meals | Mix unknown |

**Decision rules**
1. Same provider and same service bundle beats same neighborhood.
2. The two communities must differ in primary language or cultural mix. I ask the coordinator; I don't assume.
3. At least about three role-takers reachable per site, or a route to them.
4. The coordinator agrees to introductions and interpreter support.

**Order of preference**
- **First:** Lady Shaw vs. Manilatown (same provider, bundle, district). If Manilatown itself serves Chinese and Filipino elders in real numbers, it could be one site with two communities, the tightest structural control.
- **Then:** Jackie Chan vs. Richmond Senior Center (different providers, so I record differences).
- **Bigger contrast, weaker control:** Chinatown (SHE) vs. Western Addition.
- **If role-takers are scarce:** recruit through Community Living Campaign's Community Connector networks (D7, D10, D11) or Felton's Senior Companion placements (e.g. Canon Kip, Dr. George Davis, OMI Senior Center), then pair with connected non-takers at the host site.

**New flags on the first-choice pair**
- **Proximity.** 1483 Mason and 848 Kearny are close, and both are SHE. Elders may attend both. Ask whether participants overlap, because overlap shrinks the cultural contrast.
- **Housing confound.** Manilatown sits inside senior housing, so its elders may be residents, not drop-in attendees. That is a different population. Record residence status.
- **"Chinese" is not one culture.** Cantonese, Toisanese and Mandarin speakers, and different immigration cohorts, differ. Use communities as people describe themselves, and note within-group variation. Don't treat family-first as a fixed trait of a group.

### 7.2 Who I compare

| | Has a role | No role |
|---|---|---|
| **Wants one** | Fulfilled role-takers | Frustrated wanters (never asked, waitlisted, blocked by health, language, eligibility) |
| **Doesn't want one** | Reluctant holders (necessity or obligation) | Content non-takers (family, faith, friends) |

- **The grid is for sampling** (maximum variation across cells), not a theory. Categories can be revised after interviews. An empty cell is a finding.
- **Sample:** about 6–8 elders per site (roughly half role-takers), 2 staff and 1 cultural broker per site. 12–16 elders in all.
- **Why 12–16, honestly:** saturation was reached at 9–17 interviews (median 12–13) across 23 empirical tests, but one study needed 16–24 for *meaning* saturation (Hennink & Kaiser 2022, Hennink et al. 2017: ✅). Treat this as exploratory and say so. Narrow aim and rich conversations lower the number needed (Malterud et al. 2016 ◇).
- **Conditions recorded:** health and mobility, being asked, wanting a role, living arrangement, prior occupation, language, years in SF and the neighborhood, caregiving, transport and safety, digital access, **gender**.
- **Scope limit:** center participants are healthier, more social and lower-income than peers.

### 7.3 Methods

- **Access:** coordinator, introductions, snowball. No door-knocking.
- **Elders:** 30–45 min story-based conversations, audio, in their language. The role screener comes at the *end* so the categories don't steer the story (draft prompts, section 14).
- **Observation:** one program session per site, no camera.
- **Staff and brokers:** about two staff and one community or faith leader per site.
- **Interpreters:** the person who introduces a participant does not interpret. Use a neutral bilingual volunteer. Brief them to interpret fully and not summarise. Keep original-language audio. Debrief after each interview. If budget allows, a second bilingual checks two interviews per language (Squires 2009, Temple & Young 2004 ◇).

### 7.4 Rigor

- **Positionality note** (half page, before fieldwork): my relationship to each language and culture and to SF, and how that may shape what elders tell me.
- **Analysis:** read inductively first (reflexive thematic analysis, Braun & Clarke ◇), then build a case-by-variable matrix (Miles, Huberman & Saldaña ◇). Within-site contrast first, then across sites.
- **Negative cases:** actively seek cases that break P1–P3 and report them.
- **Same prompts at both sites,** and note who raised culture first, me or the elder, since interviewer prompting inflates "cultural talk."
- **Reflexive memo after each interview.**
- **Write the analysis plan (codes, matrix columns) before the first interview.**

## 8. Layer B: Dongmyo and the SF Bay Area (institutional comparison)

**Purpose.** Show what channels exist for elders to take a role, so P2 ("the site offers a channel") has an institutional backdrop. I compare *channel design*, not outcomes.

| Dimension | Dongmyo / Seoul | SF Bay Area |
|---|---|---|
| Main channel types | National senior-job scheme (public-interest, social-service, market and placement types, verify categories); senior welfare centers; senior halls and clubs | DAS Community Service Centers; Community Connector (1,530 clients, FY24–25 ◻); AmeriCorps Seniors-type programs such as Senior Companion; SCSEP; faith and community groups |
| Who asks | Fill from interviews and guidelines | Mostly the organization (63% of older volunteers ◻); then friends (24%) |
| Reward | Stipend-based scheme (fill amount from MOHW guideline) | Mostly unpaid; SCSEP is the paid-training exception (verify current rules) |
| Eligibility | Fill from MOHW guideline | Fill from DAS and SCSEP texts |
| Language and cultural fit | Largely monocultural setting (verify) | Multilingual, site-by-site; limited-English seniors use DAS at ~2× the overall rate ◻ |
| Funding stability | Large and growing (1.24:1 applicants to slots, 2026 ◻) | Fragile (SCSEP funding held up 2025 ◻) |
| What's known on uptake | 22.4% wanted the job program (2020); volunteering 2.5% (2023) ◻ | No SF count of elders in roles found ◻ |

**Method**
- Desk review of program documents and policy texts.
- Ask DAS Planning whether any survey asked about volunteering or work interest (already on the action list).
- Optional: a short email or video check-in with one or two of last year's Dongmyo staff contacts, using the same role-channel questions. Staff interviews were what worked last time.
- **Output:** a one-page channel map with a sentence on each dimension.

**Guards**
- Different eligibility, pay and definitions. Don't rank the two places.
- Set Korean and US participation figures side by side only after confirming definitions (section 6, stretch b).

## 9. The film, and how it follows from the research

**Working logline.** In two San Francisco senior centers with the same programs and different cultures, some elders take on a role and some don't. This film asks what makes the difference, and who is asking.

**Length and cast.** 10–15 min; 4–6 people drawn from across the want × have grid at both sites; one or two staff or community voices.

**Each segment answers one part of the research question**

| Film segment | Research element it answers | What the camera does | Guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. An ordinary day at each center | Same services, different culture (site logic) | Observational: rooms, meals, hands at work; no talking heads | No identifiable non-participants; no filming in housing common areas |
| 2. Two role-takers, and how it started | P2 channel and *asked* | Short interview, then the role in action | Include one who describes an informal or family role |
| 3. Two connected non-takers, and what they do instead | Grid's "no role" cells; P3 | Same format as segment 2 | No pity framing; show content non-takers as such |
| 4. Culture on screen | Cultural strength in practice | Language, food, what gets passed on, with permission | Participant chooses what is shown |
| 5. Staff or community voice | What the site offers and doesn't (Layer B) | One or two voices; optional text-only title card on how other places ask (Dongmyo) | Staff don't speak for elders |
| 6. Open ending | The gap: no source settles the link | No conclusion | End card: findings are exploratory, small number of conversations |

**Rules of the film**
- Subtitles in English and the original language; ambient sound; refusals shown respectfully.
- **Research and film stay separate.** Audio interviews first, camera second, opt-in only. People who agree to be filmed may show more agency than those who decline, so I report who declined and choose film participants across the grid.
- **The audio interviews are the data; the film is a representation of cases.** The film doesn't "prove" anything.
- **Rough-cut screening.** Show participants and site staff a rough cut before it is final; participants can withdraw or change how they appear (Birt et al. 2016 on member checking ◇).
- **Layered consent** (Appendix E). Identifiable video is treated as more than minimal risk in UK research-ethics guidance, and many participants dislike being anonymised, so people choose their own level (Wiles et al. 2008 ◻).
- **Visual method anchors:** Pink, *Doing Visual Ethnography*; Harper on visual sociology; MacDougall on observational cinema (all ◇).
- **Also deliver:** a one- to two-page findings memo and a consent log. Check whether the film can replace or must accompany the final essay or presentation.

## 10. Ethics and access

- Consent in the participant's language, read aloud if needed; nothing required to be signed on the spot.
- **Never ask about immigration status.** Fear tied to immigration status was cited as a barrier by 9% (random sample) and 19% (service-connected sample) of DAS survey respondents ◻.
- **Capacity to consent:** if unsure, check with staff; stop if the person seems distressed or confused.
- **Data:** audio on an encrypted drive, pseudonyms, named access, a stated retention period, and no sharing of unblurred footage beyond the agreed level.
- **Payment:** DAS paid $50 to its focus-group participants ◻, a benchmark if I have budget. Check each site's policy on gifts first, and keep payment from becoming pressure.
- **Instructor letter first.** Confirm whether this is a class project or research needing review (many universities treat class projects as non-research, but a public film may change that, so ask the IRB or instructor office), and whether data can be reused beyond the class.

## 14. Draft prompts (replace Appendix A/B if yours differ)

**Conversation arc (indirect, experience-based; no theory words)**
1. How did you come to this center?
2. Tell me about a normal week.
3. Something you're proud of, or a skill from earlier in your life.
4. What from your culture do you keep up here? With whom? What do you hope younger people carry on?
5. A time you decided something important. Who was part of that decision?
6. What would you like to do more of, or less of?
7. What would you want a visitor to understand about this place?

**Screener (at the end, not the start)**
1. In a normal week, is there anything you do here or nearby that other people count on you for? *(role, formal or informal)*
2. Has anyone ever asked you to take something on, here, at church, at home? Who? What happened? *(asked)*
3. If something like that were available and fit you, would you want it? What would it need to look like? *(want)*

Health and mobility come from observation and staff, not direct questions. Never ask "are you lonely?"
